# Can other loans improve an anchored default forecast?

The estimate at issue is a country’s first-year default frequency, computed over the last twelve fully mature cohorts. The model learns how far to update that estimate as more repayments become observable. Its three fitted coefficients correspond to the ordinary risk update, other loans issued alongside the pilot, and the pilot itself. Predictions concern the share of future portfolio principal associated with a first-year default. ## Reading one forecast

Let the issue-time frequency be $q$ and the refreshed frequency be $u$. If the ordinary update receives weight $a$, a 5-point increase in the frequency moves the model's forecast by $5a$ percentage points. The resulting forecast is $P=q+a(u-q)$. If both banks see $n$ other outcomes and $d$ defaults from the pilot month, their shared forecast becomes

$$
C=P+b\frac{d-nP}{20+n}.
$$

When $n>0$, this puts weight $b n/(20+n)$ on the peer default rate and the remaining weight on $P$. No peers leaves $P$ unchanged. Training chooses each weight in turn to reduce squared error on subsequent books, with a fixed penalty on the square of the weight. Every historical country–month contributes one target irrespective of book size. The loss weights defaults within that target by principal. [Fitted weights](../results/frequency_model_coefficients.csv)

Before observing the pilot, the chance assigned to its default is $\pi=(d+20u)/(n+20)$. The private slope $\gamma$ measures the association between the pilot's unexpected outcome $Y-\pi$ and the remaining error in predicting future default exposure. It can be positive or negative. For each portfolio the code restricts it to the range in which both conditional probabilities are feasible. After observing a repayment without a first-year default, the model uses $m_0=C-\gamma\pi$. After a first-year default it uses $m_1=C+\gamma(1-\pi)$. As a check, the weighted average $(1-\pi)m_0+\pi m_1$ equals $C$ for every portfolio. When computing the sensitivity with peers hidden, the training and scoring code set $n=d=0$ and re-estimate the private slope.

For example, suppose $q=20\%$, $u=15\%$, $a=0.5$, and $b=0.6$. The ordinary update gives 17.5%. If 12 of 100 measured peers defaulted, then $C=14.75\%$ and $\pi=12.5\%$. An illustrative positive private slope of 0.04 gives a forecast of 14.25% after no default and 18.25% after default. The common forecast is below the 18.18% funding cutoff, while one of the two possible observations would take it above. The average additional decision value under these hypothetical probabilities is just €0.047 per €1,000 of follow-up principal. Crossing the cutoff by a small amount leaves little money available to cover a pilot loss.

## How much can a bank offer to learn the outcome?

The preceding calculation supposes the later common records are already known. At issue the model instead draws whole pairs of ordinary and peer updates from earlier, mature country–months. It shifts both rates by the same amount on the log-odds scale to match the current $q$ and carries each observed peer count along with its peer rate. The bank averages the gain in the best available action across those states, scales by the projected future book, and adds the expected direct margin on the pilot. This assumes the historical changes are transferable and that projected exposure can be priced separately from the per-euro decision gain. Each scoring row contains the forecast gain, the expected direct margin and the two later decisions. [Forecast ledger](../results/frequency_model_episodes.csv)

Two boosting controls use the preceding study's fixed specification. One is trained on exactly the same cutoff and episodes as the new slopes. The other retains the earlier October 2022 training window. Comparing the two helps distinguish a change in fitted architecture from a change in available training history. A second within-model comparison keeps the public context identical as it hides and reveals the pilot. ## What the fitted slopes did

The June 2024 training cut supplied 277 complete episodes across 144 months. The slope for the ordinary update was 0.5483 and the peer weight was 0.5678. When peers were visible, the fitted separation in forecast risk between a defaulting and a nondefaulting pilot was **0.77 percentage points**. With peers hidden it was 4.59 points. These are slope estimates conditional on the two assumed information environments. Both have to earn their predictive value on later loans. [Public coefficients](../results/frequency_model_coefficients.csv)

All twelve primary test books had 25 measured outcomes. Brier loss moved from **0.083211** at issue to 0.079527 after the ordinary update and 0.078720 after other loans. Revealing the pilot gave 0.078726. The unadjusted refreshed frequency scored 0.078637 and the directly smoothed peer rate 0.078538. At issue, historical frequency still beat boosting refitted to the same June 2024 cutoff (0.083211 versus 0.090840). The refitted booster improved to 0.081932 on common records. In its own matched hidden/revealed comparison it scored 0.081906 and 0.081877. Both disclosure-time forecasts beat the issue-time frequency on these dates. The October 2022 booster gave 0.094073 with the pilot revealed on the identical test books. The newer training vintage also matters for this comparison. The primary test covers four months. [Full comparisons](../results/frequency_evaluation.csv)

The June 2024 Dutch episode was the only initially loss-making pilot opportunity. Historical risk was 20.86%. An ordinary update lowered the anchored forecast to 12.35%, changing the continuation action from decline to lend. Peer outcomes lowered the forecast to 7.76%, and the pilot's recorded absence of a first-year default took it to 7.73%. The €67,808 book recorded zero first-year defaults, giving +€6,780.80 in the fixed two-payoff scenario. Its cumulative cash at the archive cutoff was −€47,498.91 with €54,043.41 principal outstanding. Its cash effect arose from ordinary news that both strategies received. [Action ledger](../results/frequency_actions.csv)

With shared peers, buying this pilot was priced at a €50.57 expected direct loss against €1.17 of information and the model passed. Without peers the signal had a higher price, €46.62 against an expected loss of €43.23. Under that sensitivity the bank would issue the pilot with €3.40 expected net. The realized pilot signal left the continuation action unchanged. The first pilot's own cash was −€1,755.43 through the cutoff, with €2,343.40 outstanding. The position-26 experiment used another pilot on each of the same twelve portfolios. All first and position-26 pilots had no measured default in their first year. Excluding either a first or a 26th nondefaulting pilot therefore leaves identical peer default counts and identical anchored forecasts on every book. The alternative pilots had different contract sizes in eleven of twelve episodes and gave different monetary exposure.
