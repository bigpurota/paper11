# Did a lender actually change its decision and recover a pilot loss?

A difficult claim requires three observations in the same setting. A lender pays to fund a pilot, its repayment changes a later credit decision, and the incremental margin of that change covers the pilot's loss. For a claim about *realized* payback, the pilot itself must have lost money in realized cash flows. A pilot with a negative forecast that later pays interest is a different case. The field studies below document actual lending decisions. None reports all three observations for an individual pilot.

## A bank changed its approval policy using experimental repayment data

In Kenya, Commercial Bank of Africa ran project TOBOA from December 2013 to May 2014 for its M-Shwari product. It targeted about 100,000 previously rejected people with loan offers. The bank bore the first 4% of losses and FSD Kenya guaranteed additional losses up to KSh30 million. The case study reports that 5% of the pilot loans defaulted and KSh0.5 million was drawn from the guarantee. The bank incorporated the experimental data into its next scorecard. Acceptance rose from 40% to 47% and the reported nonperforming loan rate fell to 2%. This is direct evidence that information from exploratory lending informed an actual bank policy. The report gives neither net earnings on the TOBOA loans nor incremental later profits against a continuation of the old policy. A guarantee payout counts money paid by the guarantor. Inferring a net bank loss or its recovery from that amount would require further cash flows. The reported 17-month break-even time refers to the M-Shwari business as a whole. [FSD Kenya and FSD Africa, M-Shwari case study, pp. 6–7](https://fsdafrica.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/M-Shwari_Briefing-final_digital.pdf#page=8)

## A lender actually funded previously rejected applicants

In South Africa, a consumer finance company wanted to know whether its branches applied excessively strict underwriting. It randomly encouraged staff to reconsider some marginal rejections. Staff retained discretion and issued 172 first loans they had initially declined. Discounted receipts on those 172 loans were R221,315.01 on R175,581.39 advanced. After the authors' allowance for staff time, estimated profit was R34,643.62, or R201.42 per funded marginal loan. This is the direct financial return on the experimental loans as a group. It does not measure what their repayment histories earned on later applicants. [Karlan and Zinman, Table 6, p. 459](https://s3.amazonaws.com/fieldexperiments-papers2/papers/00281.pdf#page=27)

Management judged the estimated return sufficient. Footnote 13 explains that a merger occurred before the results could inform company policy. The researchers lacked access to the acquiring company's underwriting policy and records. Follow-up data also ended before final collections on repeat loans. This rules out attributing the R201.42 to post-pilot learning. [Karlan and Zinman, p. 437, footnote 13, and p. 460](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhp092)

## What other lenders actually changed

| Setting | Documented change | What the financial evidence establishes |
|---|---|---|
| An African small-business bank | From May through August 2015 the bank piloted credit-bureau scoring at 14 of 28 randomly assigned branches. In September the bank rolled the system out to all branches. The trial covered 26,503 applications. | The study estimates that the new process rejected about six percentage points more applications. Its business case assumes operating-cost savings. The rollout documents a real policy decision at the level of a screening system. The study does not trace it to a particular loan's repayment or report that loan's payback. [Gietzen, §2.1, Figure 1, §4.4](https://www.garp.org/hubfs/Whitepapers/a1Z1W0000054xWbUAI.pdf) |
| Kenyan digital lending | A practitioner described an early underwriting experiment with KES 500 credit lines and a repayment rate recalled at roughly 20%. The researchers describe subsequent customer research and a move to adjusting credit limits according to borrower risk. | This is a first-hand account of disappointing repayments and changes to credit practice. It supplies neither a reconciled pilot loss nor downstream profits relative to a decision made without that information. [Mwesigwa, Jackson and Csikszentmihalyi, §5.2.2](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790924) |
| A US auto finance company | In June 2001 the lender used credit-bureau and historical company information to set minimum down payments and determine which cars it would finance. Higher-risk applicants faced larger minimum down payments. | Estimated net revenue rose from $751 to $1,070 per applicant in a before-and-after comparison. The estimate concerns the whole underwriting change and is subject to contemporaneous changes. It does not identify the value of any individual experimental loan. ## What would establish payback in our setting?

Time-stamped underwriting logs could document the forecast at issuance, the pilot cash flow, the later decision under the usual information, and the decision after adding the pilot result. A bank can log a shadow recommendation with the pilot outcome withheld. This verifies that the signal changed an action. To estimate the resulting money, the no-signal comparison also needs credible outcomes. For example, a bank could randomly assign comparable portfolios to a funded-pilot policy or to its usual no-pilot policy and measure both portfolios' total cash margins. Alternatively, funding a pilot in both arms and randomly withholding its outcome in one arm estimates the gross gain from the information. In that design, add the pilot cash margin once to compare funding a pilot with foregoing it. Even with randomized access, the two potential profits for the very same borrower cannot both be observed. A retrospective calculation on funded borrowers instead states what a specified rule would have earned under its cash-flow and comparability assumptions.
