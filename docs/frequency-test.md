# Did the frequency correction change a lending decision?

Ordinary public news changed one of twelve continuation decisions on the June–September 2024 test. The other borrowers' records improved the anchored model's average probability forecast, and the pilot’s own flag changed zero lending actions. The explicit pooling formula for peer defaults gave the lowest average Brier loss. The protocol fixed the rules before the paired endpoints were opened. [Protocol commit](https://github.com/bigpurota/paper11/commit/fff202a) · [Open source and exposure definitions](frequency-panel.md)

## What the next four months showed

We fitted the model on 277 mature country–month episodes across 144 distinct months using labels available by June 2024. At issue it starts at the measured risk in its country. At disclosure it applies 54.8% of the change in the ordinary frequency and 56.8% of the remaining, count-adjusted peer innovation. With peers, changing the pilot flag from no first-year default to default shifts the forecast by at most 0.771 percentage points. Without peers that fitted slope rises to 4.59 percentage points. A prediction must cross the 18.18% threshold to change the action. [How each correction is estimated](frequency-model.md) · [Fitted slopes](../results/frequency_model_coefficients.csv)

Brier loss measures forecast error on individual first-year default indicators. Each later book weights its 25 loans by principal; the twelve books receive equal weight. The independently fitted common-only boosting forecast and its branch mixture are slightly different predictors. The frequency correction has exactly the same common forecast before revelation either way. [Scores and comparisons](../results/frequency_evaluation.csv)

Common news reduced the anchored model's Brier by **0.004491** versus the frequency at issue, consisting of 0.003684 from the ordinary update and 0.000807 from peers. The common-news gain stayed positive after omitting any single month (0.001397 to 0.006404), though it was negative in September alone. Across the four Dutch books it was +0.014277, in Estonia +0.001100 and in Finland −0.001903. Revealing the pilot reduced Brier by **−0.000006** overall and the sign changed across months. The pure, observable peer frequency beat the learned common correction by 0.000182. The older October 2022 boosting model scored 0.094073 with the pilot revealed; refreshing its training data brought that value to 0.081877. Comparisons at the same training date isolate the design more closely. There are just two calendar quarters. We report the entire window and the four leave-one-month-out means as descriptive evidence. [Month, country and alternative-feed tables](../results/frequency_evaluation.csv)

## The Dutch decision

The historical default frequency put the June 2024 Dutch candidate's future book at **20.86%**, so the frequency-only rule would decline it. At disclosure the ordinary frequency had fallen to **5.34%**. Applying the learned 54.8% correction moved risk to **12.35%**, crossing the break-even threshold and switching the action to lend. The shared peer update moved it to **7.76%**. Revealing that the pilot had no recorded first-year default moved it to **7.73%**. The last two updates left the action at lend. The same-cutoff boosting model with the same shared records and the anchored model chose the same action on all twelve books. [Candidate-level forecasts and actions](../results/frequency_model_episodes.csv) · [Signed changes](../results/frequency_actions.csv)

The 25 next Dutch loans were actually issued, with €67,808 in principal and zero measured first-year defaults. Lending earned a hypothetical **+€6,780.80** under the fixed one-year payoff terms. The November 2026 ledger instead showed **−€47,498.91** in cash received less principal issued, with **€54,043.41** principal still outstanding. A cash position during repayment and a payoff based on a one-year flag answer different questions. The rule switching from decline to lend is a replay of archived positions. The source does not observe the decisions a real lender would have made under either information arrangement. [Action and cash accounting](../results/frequency_action_summary.csv)

## Would the bank buy that pilot?

Only the Dutch June episode entered the initial direct-loss screen. With peers expected to be available, the primary model valued its €2,704 pilot at **−€50.57** in direct margin and **+€1.17** from better subsequent decisions. Both it and the earlier size-aware rule skipped the pilot. If peers were unavailable to both strategies, the anchored model priced the same signal at €46.62, against an expected direct loss of €43.23, and would have issued. In the archived outcome that loan's cumulative direct cash was −€1,755.43 and revealing its flag left follow-up lending unchanged. [Ex-ante pricing](../results/frequency_model_episodes.csv) · [Issue choices, cash and scenario payoffs](../results/frequency_policy.csv)

The alternative candidate at position 26 uses the same twelve books and had no previously scored pilot identity. It also had zero first-year pilot defaults. Swapping the two nondefaulting loans within each cohort preserved the peer event counts, so the anchored risk forecasts and continuation decisions coincide. The offered principal still changed the economics. The earlier size-aware rule selected the €936 Dutch alternative, whose observed direct cash was −€435.80. Primary and alternative results cannot be added together. Eight of the twelve primary candidates had previously featured among earlier follow-up loans. None of the 300 continuation positions had been scored in those earlier studies. All 300 first-year flags were measured, so worst- and best-case bounds for the primary Brier gains coincide. With four pilot months, one direct-loss opportunity and no realized pilot defaults, the result gives little information about the branch where a pilot actually defaults.
